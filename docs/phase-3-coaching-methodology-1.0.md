# ATENA Coaching Methodology 1.0

**Status:** development specification  
**Branch:** `phase-3/coaching-methodology`  
**Purpose:** establish the coaching methodology that must guide assessment, programme design, session review and adaptation in ATENA Coach.

This document is a product and coaching specification. It is not a clinical assessment protocol, a diagnostic tool, or evidence that the current application already implements every requirement.

## 1. Coaching principles

1. Assess only what can change a coaching decision. Do not burden users with a large generic test battery.
2. Interpret movement in context: experience, task, range of motion, control, symptoms, confidence and available equipment.
3. A movement observation is not a diagnosis and cannot identify a specific injured tissue.
4. Prescribe the smallest effective and sustainable intervention; preserve training that is well tolerated.
5. Progression is earned through repeatable performance and recovery, not applied automatically because a calendar week has passed.
6. Pain is meaningful feedback. It must be explored and used to adapt training, not ignored and not automatically treated as proof of injury.
7. The user-facing experience should be conversational and concise. The underlying decision logic may be detailed, but the user should not have to complete unnecessary forms.

## 2. Main training goals

### 2.1 Strength and muscle development

For most users, strength and hypertrophy are one combined goal: **strength and muscle development**. The programme develops both through appropriate exercise selection, sufficient weekly stimulus, progressive overload, controlled execution and recovery.

Only for highly experienced users, or when the user explicitly prioritises one quality, may ATENA separate strength-specialisation and hypertrophy-specialisation blocks. Such specialisation must be justified by training history, available time, recovery and the stated priority.

### 2.2 Functional fitness

Functional fitness is an integrated goal, not a synonym for conditioning or random MetCons. Depending on the person and their needs, it combines:

- strength;
- muscular and cardiorespiratory endurance;
- power and speed-strength;
- mobility;
- balance and coordination;
- the ability to perform relevant everyday and recreational tasks.

The proportions are individual. Not every programme needs to train every quality equally in every session. Conditioning is programmed as a component of this integrated goal, not offered as a default standalone goal.

### 2.3 Body-weight management / fat loss

The goal is reduction of excess body fat while preserving or improving muscle mass, strength and function. Training should combine:

- resistance training as the anchor;
- appropriate endurance work;
- optional, well-dosed circuits, supersets or light MetCon formats where they fit the person's capacity and recovery;
- sustainable nutrition guidance.

ATENA should provide general, practical nutrition recommendations: a moderate and sustainable energy deficit, adequate protein, nutrient-dense foods, appropriate meal structure, hydration and realistic habits. It must not promise spot reduction, prescribe crash diets, or provide medical nutrition therapy. Medical conditions, eating-disorder risk, pregnancy, medication-related concerns or other complex needs require appropriate professional input.

## 3. Initial movement assessment

### 3.1 Five core movement tasks

The default movement screen should use five recognisable tasks, explained in plain Slovenian:

1. **Počep**
2. **Izpadni korak**
3. **Predklon z nevtralnim položajem hrbtenice**
4. **Skleca**
5. **Potisk nad glavo** (initially assess the controlled overhead position/reach; do not turn this automatically into a maximal strength test)

The screen should be scaled to the person's ability. A wall, bench, raised hand position, support or reduced range may be used where appropriate. The aim is to observe the person's current capacity, not to make them fail a difficult test.

A separate pulling test is **not required in the basic movement screen**. The push-up and overhead press provide useful information for the initial movement conversation, while a pulling movement remains an essential programming pattern. ATENA must still select appropriate rows, pulldowns, pull-ups or other pulling exercises according to equipment, ability and goal. Assessment coverage must not be confused with programme balance.

### 3.2 What to observe

**Počep**
- comfortable and controlled depth;
- foot contact and visible foot/knee/trunk strategy;
- balance and confidence;
- whether range or control changes with a simple regression;
- symptoms during and after the task.

**Izpadni korak**
- control and balance on both sides;
- tolerance of the range and loading;
- trunk and lower-limb strategy;
- differences between sides without assuming that asymmetry is pathology;
- symptoms and confidence.

**Predklon z nevtralnim položajem hrbtenice**
- ability to perform a controlled forward bend while maintaining the person's natural spinal curves;
- hip and trunk coordination;
- whether the person can understand and reproduce the task;
- symptoms, range and confidence.

A light dowel, broomstick or similar implement can provide tactile and visual feedback at the head, thoracic region and sacrum. The cue is to maintain a comfortable **neutral spinal position**, not to force the back into a geometrically straight line. Avoid describing the task simply as “straight back”.

**Skleca**
- select an appropriate incline or floor variation;
- observe the body line and trunk control;
- observe scapular movement and control, shoulder position and chest position;
- note the bottom position (relative shoulder/elbow position, chest and scapulae) and the top position (controlled finish without forcing the shoulders);
- observe symptoms and whether a simpler variation changes them.

**Potisk nad glavo**
- observe comfortable active arm elevation and control;
- note shoulder flexion/reach, rib-cage and chest position, scapular movement and compensatory trunk extension;
- distinguish available range from the ability to control that range;
- do not force end range or use a loaded overhead press when symptoms or control make that inappropriate.

### 3.3 Additional positions

Use two simple positions as complementary observations, not as standalone diagnostic tests:

- **Globok čep** – observe the combined strategy and tolerance of the position, including ankle dorsiflexion, knee and hip flexion, balance and trunk position.
- **Klečanje s sedenjem na petah** – observe tolerance of knee flexion and the combined ankle/foot position, including plantar-flexed ankle positioning.

These positions may help identify where further questions or a modified exercise choice are useful. They do not isolate one joint or prove the cause of a restriction. In particular, do not infer that a limitation is caused solely by ankle mobility, knee mobility, hip structure or a specific tissue based on one visual observation.

### 3.4 How to collect and interpret the assessment

- Explain each task and show a simple demonstration.
- Ask the user to perform only a few comfortable repetitions or a short hold; do not fatigue them.
- Use a dowel/broomstick for the forward-bend feedback where available.
- Record the observed task, variation, range, control, confidence and symptoms.
- If video is used, obtain explicit consent and do not require it for programme access.
- Prefer the user's report plus observable task performance; do not claim precise joint angles or diagnoses from ordinary phone video.
- Repeat the same task and variation at reassessment to make comparisons meaningful.
- A task can be “not assessed” or “uncertain”; do not force a pass/fail label.

The output should be actionable coaching notes, for example: “počep do udobne globine je nadzorovan; pri večji globini se dvigujejo pete; začni z ustrezno regresijo in postopno preverjaj toleranco.” It must not say “ankle dysfunction” or infer a diagnosis.

## 4. Baseline performance measures

Movement observations and performance tests are separate layers. Add only measures relevant to the selected goal and the person's experience.

- **Strength and muscle development:** suitable submaximal set performance, load, repetitions, estimated repetitions in reserve (RIR), technique and repeatability. Do not require a beginner to perform a 1RM test.
- **Functional fitness:** select a small number of repeatable measures for relevant qualities, such as a controlled sit-to-stand, step/carry task, balance task or a submaximal conditioning measure. Do not test every quality just because it exists in the goal definition.
- **Body-weight management:** where the user wants to track it, use optional body mass and/or waist trend, training performance, adherence and relevant wellbeing markers. Explain normal day-to-day variation and avoid making scale weight the only measure of success.

Every baseline measure must have a clear reason, a safe protocol, and a plausible way it will change a programme decision. If it has no decision value, omit it.

## 5. Pain and symptom-informed coaching

### 5.1 Core principle

Pain is information from the person and their body. It is not noise to be ignored, but it is also not, by itself, a diagnosis or proof of tissue damage. ATENA must respond with curiosity, empathy and proportionate caution.

Do not require every user to be completely pain-free before any training can be considered. Do not assume the absence of pain when the user has not explicitly answered. Do not infer safety clearance from silence or from unrelated answers.

### 5.2 Minimum clarification when pain is reported

Ask one concise, relevant question at a time and retain the answers. Establish:

- location and side;
- which movement, exercise or daily task brings it on;
- when it appears (during the task, immediately after, later that day or the following day);
- intensity and character as described by the user, including whether it is new, familiar, changing or worsening;
- whether it changes movement, strength, range, confidence or normal function;
- whether there are other concerning symptoms or relevant medical restrictions.

Where useful, record a simple 0–10 self-rating, but never use the number alone to decide whether training is safe. Compare the person's response with their own baseline and the trend over time.

### 5.3 Training adaptation

Use a local, exercise-specific response before changing the whole programme:

1. Stop or modify the provoking task if symptoms are sharp, escalating, unfamiliar or causing loss of control.
2. Explore a tolerable regression: reduce load, range, speed, complexity or volume; change the implement or position; or select a different exercise that trains the same useful capacity.
3. Preserve unaffected exercises and training qualities where reasonably appropriate.
4. Ask how the modified option feels during the session and how the person responds afterwards and the next day.
5. If symptoms settle and function remains stable, progress gradually and individually; do not force progression.
6. If symptoms persist, recur, worsen or materially affect function, recommend assessment by an appropriately qualified health professional.

The system must not automatically remove all resistance training because one movement is uncomfortable. Equally, it must not encourage a person to push through worsening or concerning pain.

### 5.4 Referral and urgent warning signs

ATENA must not diagnose. It should recommend prompt medical assessment for concerning, new, severe or worsening symptoms, substantial loss of function, significant swelling, inability to bear weight, new neurological symptoms or other signs that warrant clinical evaluation. Possible emergency symptoms (for example chest pain, severe breathing difficulty, fainting, sudden major weakness or other acute severe symptoms) require emergency guidance appropriate to the user's location.

The exact referral wording and red-flag rules require a separately reviewed safety policy. A numeric pain threshold must never override a red flag or substitute for clinical judgement.

## 6. Programme design rules

The programme generator must combine:

- primary goal and user priority;
- training experience and history;
- movement observations and symptom constraints;
- available equipment and safe setup;
- realistic session duration and weekly availability;
- recovery, preferences and adherence;
- the exercise catalogue, including explicit regressions and progressions.

Exercise selection should prioritise relevant multi-joint movements and fundamental movement patterns. Single-joint work may be added when it serves a clear purpose, especially for more experienced users or when additional volume is appropriate. Do not add generic stability exercises by default; use them when assessment or feedback identifies a specific need.

For each exercise, the system should internally know:
- why it was selected;
- the intended movement pattern and training effect;
- the suitable starting variation;
- clear regression and progression options;
- equipment and setup requirements;
- range, tempo, sets, repetitions or time, rest and effort target;
- symptoms or technique signals that should trigger a change.

Prescriptions must reflect experience. Beginners generally need manageable, repeatable sets with enough repetitions to learn the movement and build capacity; avoid defaulting to heavy low-repetition prescriptions. Tempo and a large **controlled, tolerable** range of motion matter. Do not chase maximum range at the expense of control or symptom tolerance.

Session duration is flexible. Preserve the main training stimulus and use realistic transitions and rest periods rather than filling a fixed time with too many exercises.

## 7. Session feedback and adaptation loop

After a session, collect only the information needed to make the next decision. Prefer a short conversational check-in:

- completed, partly completed or not completed;
- actual load/repetitions/time when relevant;
- perceived effort or RIR;
- movement quality and confidence;
- pain or discomfort during and after;
- recovery/readiness and any practical reason for changes.

Interpret incomplete sessions correctly: lack of time is not the same as excessive effort; a missed session is not automatically poor recovery; and an unanswered pain question is not confirmation of no pain.

Decision examples:

- **Good execution + appropriate effort + normal recovery:** maintain or progress the relevant exercise according to its progression rule.
- **Target completed but effort unexpectedly high or technique deteriorated:** hold or reduce the affected exercise's dose; do not progress it yet.
- **Repeated poor recovery across sessions:** review total weekly stress, volume, intensity, schedule and recovery context.
- **Pain linked to one exercise:** apply the symptom-informed local adaptation; preserve unaffected training where appropriate.
- **Insufficient or ambiguous feedback:** ask one useful follow-up or maintain the current conservative prescription; do not invent certainty.

The analysis engine should explain internally which evidence supports a decision, what remains uncertain, and what signal will be checked next. Programme-wide changes should be deliberate and transparent, not hidden automatic rewrites.

## 8. Reassessment

Reassess when there is a meaningful reason: after a planned training block, after a relevant change in symptoms or capacity, when progress stalls, or when the user's goal or circumstances change.

Repeat the same movement task and baseline protocol where possible. Compare like with like. Reassessment should answer:
- what has changed;
- what has remained stable;
- whether the original programme assumptions were correct;
- what should be progressed, maintained, modified or referred.

Do not retest everything at every check-in.

## 9. Required quality and safety tests

The implementation must include automated tests and human-reviewed conversation cases covering at least:

1. The default movement screen contains the five core tasks and the two complementary positions.
2. The forward-bend terminology is “predklon z nevtralnim položajem hrbtenice”; dowel feedback is supported.
3. No standalone pulling assessment is required, while pulling exercises remain eligible and appropriately represented in programmes.
4. A movement observation never produces a medical diagnosis or a definitive claim about the cause of a restriction.
5. Deep squat and kneeling-on-heels observations do not claim to isolate one joint.
6. Strength and muscle development are one default goal; specialisation is conditional on advanced experience or explicit priority.
7. Conditioning is a component of functional fitness, not a default standalone primary goal.
8. Fat-loss plans include resistance training, suitable endurance work and safe, sustainable nutrition guidance.
9. Pain is clarified and recorded without assuming either injury or absence of symptoms.
10. Pain adaptation is specific to the provoking movement where possible and preserves unaffected training.
11. Red flags override ordinary progression and trigger appropriate referral/emergency guidance.
12. Missing or ambiguous feedback never becomes a false “pain-free” or “safe to progress” conclusion.
13. Regression/progression, equipment compatibility, realistic session duration and user experience remain independently quality-checked.

## 10. Implementation sequence

1. Map the current questionnaire, profile model, movement assessment, exercise catalogue, pain guardrails, session feedback and adaptation endpoints.
2. Identify existing capabilities and gaps before changing code; avoid duplicating existing modules.
3. Implement the movement-assessment model and user-facing explanations.
4. Align goal taxonomy and programme selection with section 2.
5. Integrate pain and symptom feedback into a single conservative decision loop.
6. Connect session analysis to explicit programme revision and reassessment.
7. Add regression tests and synthetic Slovenian multi-turn scenarios.
8. Run offline tests first; then a controlled live test on the isolated pilot only if credentials and free-tier limits permit.
9. Report technical test results separately from human-rated coaching quality. Do not deploy, change provider/model, or enable paid services without explicit approval.
