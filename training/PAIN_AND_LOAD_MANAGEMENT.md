# ATENA coaching policy: pain, load and capacity

## Source hierarchy and scope

This policy translates the coaching principles in the Functional Fitness manual and the FZS congress seminar *Trajno fit* into rules for exercise selection and adaptation. It is a training-coach policy, not a clinical assessment or treatment protocol.

Where an older teaching phrase could be read as an absolute rule (for example, any pain means a movement is unacceptable), use the more nuanced *Trajno fit* framework: pain is relevant information, but it is not by itself a diagnosis or proof of tissue damage. Never ignore symptoms, and never infer a diagnosis from a movement observation.

## Demand, capacity and response

For a movement task, consider the relationship between:
- the mechanical and coordination demands of the task;
- the person's current capacity (strength, muscle mass, ROM, control, coordination, stability, tissue tolerance, conditioning and recovery);
- the dose applied;
- the response during the session and afterwards, including the following day.

The apparent limiting factor is not automatically an injured tissue. Identify which task demand or dose is currently least tolerated; do not diagnose the cause.

## What to monitor

When a person reports pain or discomfort, establish:
- where and when it occurs;
- which movement, position or activity provokes it;
- the load, effort, ROM, volume, frequency and speed at which it appears;
- whether it changes during the session;
- how the person feels later and the following day;
- whether symptoms are new, severe, worsening, unusual or accompanied by other concerning signs.

Distinguish muscular effort, fatigue and ordinary post-exercise soreness from pain. Do not use a single pain score as the only decision variable.

## Modification sequence

Do not automatically remove the movement pattern. When appropriate and within coaching scope, consider changing one or more variables:
1. external load or resistance;
2. range of motion and task position;
3. repetitions, sets, total volume or frequency;
4. effort/intensity and rest;
5. tempo or movement speed;
6. support, stability demands or exercise variation.

A lower-load, controlled preparation set may be useful when it is comfortable and the person's response supports continuing. If symptoms persist, increase, or the response is unfavourable, further modify the task or stop that exercise for now. Do not force progression.

## Progression

Progression is not limited to adding weight. Depending on the goal and response, capacity can be developed through gradual changes in load, ROM, volume, frequency, speed, coordination or task complexity. Reassess the response before increasing demands. ROM is a tool, not dogma: full ROM is often useful for strength, muscle and function, but the tolerable range is individual and can be developed progressively. Capacity is dynamic; fatigue, sleep, stress, pain and prior loading can change what a person tolerates on a given day.

## Referral and professional boundaries

ATENA and the coach must not diagnose, prescribe medical treatment or claim to identify the injured structure from symptoms or movement appearance. New, severe, progressive or unusual symptoms, neurological symptoms, or other concerning signs require referral to an appropriate healthcare professional. When uncertain, pause the relevant coaching decision and recommend professional assessment.

## Technical quality

Technique means an effective execution for the person's task, goal and current capacity—not conformity to one universal visual ideal. Modify technique when it improves effectiveness or tolerance, not simply to make every person look identical. A compensation is not automatically an error: consider why it appears, how large it is, whether it is controlled, whether it is symptomatic and whether it exceeds current capacity. Do not demand perfect symmetry; build sufficient, useful and balanced capacity for the person's tasks.

Biomechanics helps explain where and how mechanical demands arise. It does not, by itself, determine whether a movement is safe. The same exercise can create different demands in different people.

## Implications for ATENA

- Record symptoms and response as context for coaching decisions, not as diagnostic labels.
- Keep pain adaptation separate from the goal-based programme generator.
- Do not automatically ban squats, lunges, predklon, pushing, pulling, overhead work or deep ROM because a user reports a symptom.
- Do not automatically prescribe the same regression for everyone with the same body-region complaint.
- Do not progress solely because a set was completed; consider symptoms, effort, movement control and delayed response.
- If the user reports red flags or symptoms outside coaching scope, do not continue automated exercise experimentation.

## Movement observation: avoid false conclusions

- A visible deviation or compensation is an observation, not a diagnosis. Do not infer the underlying cause from one repetition or one position.
- A single sign (for example, the shoulder appearing forward in the bottom of a push-up) does not by itself prove scapular immobility, poor scapular control or restricted shoulder internal rotation. Gather additional task-specific information.
- The scapula must move. Protraction and retraction are normal parts of pushing tasks; upward rotation and some elevation are normal components of overhead arm movement. Do not rigidly pin the shoulder blades or treat all elevation as faulty. Assess whether movement is appropriate, controlled, tolerable and coordinated with the task.
- The ribcage and trunk are not fixed structures. Assess whether the person can organize and control them for the task, without unwanted or excessive compensation; do not force one static posture across all movements.
- Separate available range, active range, control through range and symptom tolerance. A limited position can reflect mobility, strength, coordination, confidence, unfamiliarity or symptoms; observation alone cannot establish which factor is responsible.
